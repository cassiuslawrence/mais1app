Somewhere between "just sign and drive off" and "refuse the car and walk away" is where most
delivery-day decisions actually live, and the internet doesn't do a great job explaining the
middle ground. This guide is that middle ground: a calm framework for deciding what to do when
you find something at handover, built around the fact that refusing delivery is a real option
—  but rarely the *best* one.

**TL;DR:**

- **Refuse** for anything touching safety, function, or that you can't confirm will actually
  get fixed
- **Document and accept** for anything cosmetic — Tesla can fix it after delivery just as well
  as before
- Refusing usually means **weeks of delay** for a new unit or repair slot
- The goal either way is the same: a **written, acknowledged record** of the problem before
  you drive off

## What "refusing delivery" actually means

You're not obligated to sign for a car you haven't had a chance to look at. In most markets,
declining to accept the vehicle at handover is a legitimate option — you simply don't sign,
and the delivery center processes it as an incomplete or refused delivery. What happens after
that (a new date, a different VIN, escalation, or in rarer cases a refund) depends on your
contract and local consumer protection law, which vary enough by country and state that it's
worth reading your delivery agreement *before* you're standing in the parking lot deciding.

## The framework: safety and function vs. cosmetics

The single question that should drive the decision: **can this be fixed after I drive away, and
will Tesla actually fix it?**

- **Cosmetic issues** — panel gaps, paint texture, a trim rattle, a scuff — are, by definition,
  things a service center can address later without the defect getting worse or costing you
  anything if it's documented at handover. These are almost never worth refusing over.
- **Safety and function issues** — an active warning light that doesn't clear, a safety system
  that doesn't engage, visible structural or mechanical damage, a system that's clearly not
  working as intended — are a different category. If you can't drive the car with confidence
  right now, or if it's not clear the problem will actually get fixed once you've already taken
  the car home, that's when refusing (or asking the delivery center to hold the car until it's
  resolved) makes sense.

## Why most people are better off documenting and accepting

Refusing delivery restarts a process — you go back into a queue for another unit or a repair
slot, and that's realistically **weeks**, not days. For anything cosmetic, you're trading real
time for a problem that gets fixed exactly the same way either way: Tesla schedules the repair.
The only thing that changes is whether you're driving the car while you wait.

Documenting and accepting gets you the same leverage as refusing — a written, dated, Tesla-
acknowledged problem — without the delay. The delivery center has a strong incentive to resolve
a documented issue quickly while it's fresh and undeniable; that incentive is what you're
actually relying on, not the act of refusing itself.

## How to document instead of refuse

1. **Photograph the issue** from an angle that shows both the defect and its location on the
   car, before you sign anything.
2. **Say it out loud to the delivery advisor** and ask them to note it in the delivery record
   or send you something in writing — an email, a note on your order, anything with a
   timestamp.
3. **Ask specifically how and when it will be addressed** — a service appointment, a part
   order, a callback — rather than accepting a vague "we'll take care of it."
4. **Keep your own copy.** Delivery centers are busy; your photos and notes are the version of
   the record you control.

## When to actually walk away

Refusing is the right call when the alternative is driving a car you can't trust, or when the
delivery center can't or won't acknowledge a real problem in writing. If a safety-related
warning won't clear, if a system doesn't respond during a test at the delivery center, or if
you're being pushed to sign before you've had a real chance to look — those are reasonable
lines to hold. Trust the pattern from every other decision in this guide: **document first,
and let the severity of what you find decide the rest.**

## The best version of this decision is one you never have to make under pressure

The stress of delivery-day decisions comes almost entirely from making them on the spot, with
a line of other buyers waiting and an advisor who's seen a hundred deliveries today. Knowing
in advance what you're looking for — and having somewhere to log it the moment you see it —
turns "do I refuse this car?" from a panic decision into a documented, calm one. That's the
whole premise behind TSL Check: a structured pass through the car, with photos and notes
attached to each point, so you walk in with a plan instead of winging it.
