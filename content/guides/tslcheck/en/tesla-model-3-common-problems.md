The Model 3 has been Tesla's volume seller for years, and the sheer number on the road means
two things: most of the quirks are well documented, and a few issues come up often enough —
at delivery on a new car, and on the used market — to be worth checking specifically before
money changes hands.

This guide covers the problems Model 3 buyers most commonly report, based on the inspection
checklist behind the TSL Check app. For each one: what it looks like, why it matters, and a
quick way to spot it. It also covers what changed with the **2023+ "Highland" refresh** and
how the **RWD, Long Range and Performance** variants differ. None of this is Tesla-bashing —
factory variation exists in every brand. It's about knowing what's normal, what isn't, and
catching the difference while it's still someone else's problem to fix.

**TL;DR — what Model 3 buyers most often report:**

- Uneven panel gaps (doors, trunk, hood/frunk)
- Paint defects: thin edges, orange peel, transport chips
- Trunk and interior trim rattles
- Wind noise from door seals
- Highland-specific: light bar alignment, rear screen, acoustic glass quirks
- Screen warnings and software alerts that shouldn't be there at handover
- Tire and brake wear (used-buyer focus, more pronounced on Performance)

## Uneven panel gaps

**The single most-reported Model 3 issue, new or used.** Doors, trunk lid and hood/frunk
should sit with gaps that look consistent from side to side. A door gap noticeably wider at
the top than the bottom points to a door misaligned on its hinges; a trunk or hood gap clearly
wider on one side than the other is a factory fit issue.

Why it matters: beyond looks, misaligned panels are a leading cause of wind noise at highway
speed, and they're the first thing a future buyer's eye lands on. Small, *even* gaps are
normal — asymmetry is the tell.

*Quick check: stand square to the front and compare the hood/frunk gap left vs. right, then
walk each door comparing its gap top vs. bottom, and do the same at the trunk.*

## Paint: thin edges, orange peel and transport marks

**Some texture is normal — thin paint and drips are not.** "Orange peel" (a light dimpled
texture in the clear coat) exists to some degree on most factory-painted cars, Tesla included.
What's worth documenting: texture visible from more than a meter away, frozen drips along the
lower edges of bumpers and doors, chips from the protective transport film, or a panel whose
color or metallic flake doesn't match its neighbors (a possible repaint).

Thin paint on door edges is a separately known Tesla issue — exposed metal there can corrode
over the years.

Why it matters: paint is a resale-value item, and edge corrosion is a slow problem that starts
the day you take delivery.

*Quick check: compare adjacent panels from about a meter away in daylight, then open each door
and look along its edges.*

## Trunk and interior trim rattles

**A loose trunk liner or unclipped trim panel is the classic source of a Model 3 rattle.**
Interior rattles are one of the most-reported Model 3 gripes — most often traced to the trunk
area, the dashboard, or a door panel that isn't fully clipped in. Trim that flexes or clicks
under light finger pressure may not be seated all the way.

Why it matters: a rattle is easy to miss on a quiet test drive and maddening to live with
daily; it's a quick fix at a delivery center and a slower, appointment-based one afterward.

*Quick check: press gently along trim edges and around the trunk liner, and note any rattle
on your test drive over rough pavement.*

## Wind noise from door seals

**A pinched or loose door seal is the classic source of highway wind noise on a Model 3.** The
rubber should run continuously around each opening and compress evenly when the door closes.
Sections that are folded, loose, or torn will whistle at speed — and can let water in during
heavy rain.

Why it matters: wind-noise complaints are among the most common Model 3 service visits, and
the cause is often a seal that could have been spotted at delivery.

*Quick check: open each door and run your eyes and fingers around the full length of the
seal.*

## What changed with the Highland refresh (2023+)

The refreshed Model 3 ("Highland") brought a redesigned front and rear light bar, a rear
passenger touchscreen, acoustic glass for a noticeably quieter cabin, ventilated front seats
on higher trims, and revised interior trim and ambient lighting. Two practical notes:

- **Early-build quirks.** As with any first-year revision, the earliest Highland builds are
  the ones to inspect most carefully — confirm the rear screen and its climate/media controls
  work as intended, and that the software shows no pending updates or alerts.
- **The acoustic glass is a real, noticeable upgrade** — if you're comparing a Highland to a
  pre-refresh car, don't mistake the quieter cabin for something being "off" on the older one;
  it's a genuine spec difference, not a defect on either car.

*Quick check on a Highland: cycle the front and rear light bars, test the rear screen's
controls, and confirm no pending software alerts.*

## RWD, Long Range and Performance: what differs

The variant changes what's worth inspecting closely more than whether the car is sound:

- **Rear-Wheel Drive** — single motor, smaller battery, the volume seller. Confirm the rated
  range matches what's advertised for the trim.
- **Long Range** — dual motor. Check that all-wheel drive engages smoothly and there are no
  drive-unit warnings.
- **Performance** — bigger brakes, stickier (and pricier) tires, lowered/firmer suspension,
  unique wheels. Inspect tire wear and brake condition closely; a Performance's consumables
  are a real invoice, and curb-rashed wheels are common on a used one.

A firmer ride and more road noise are characteristic of the Model 3 (especially Performance)
— that's the car's design, not a defect.

## What's actually normal

Calibration, not paranoia. Generally considered normal on a Model 3: small and *even* panel
gaps, a light degree of orange peel, a firm ride, some road and tire noise, and minor software
quirks that clear after an update. The pattern that matters is **asymmetry and persistence**
— one side different from the other, or an issue that doesn't go away.

## If you find something

On a **new** car, photograph anything you find before you sign, from an angle that shows both
the defect and its location, and report it to the delivery team while you're still there.
Tesla has historically allowed a short window to report cosmetic issues, but the specifics
have changed over time and vary by market — confirm the current policy at handover rather than
assuming one. On a **used** car, the same documented list is your negotiation: cosmetic and
wear items are numbers you can subtract; an undisclosed repaint, unexplained tire wear, or
paperwork that doesn't line up are reasons to renegotiate or walk. Either way, the safest
moment is always the same: before you drive away.
