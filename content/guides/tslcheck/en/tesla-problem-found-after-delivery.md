You're home, the adrenaline of delivery day has worn off, and there it is — a scratch you
swear wasn't there, or a rattle you didn't notice on the short test drive. The first reaction
for most people is some version of "did I miss my chance?" The honest answer: you have fewer
cards than you would have had at the delivery center, but you're not out of the game, and how
you report it from here matters more than how quickly you technically caught it.

**TL;DR:**

- You can still report anything, anytime — driving away doesn't end your ability to get things
  fixed
- What you lose over time is **leverage**, not eligibility — a documented, same-day report is
  simply harder to dispute than one from weeks later
- **Cosmetic** issues (scratches, gaps, paint) get harder to attribute the later you report
  them
- **Functional/safety** issues are judged on whether they're happening now, largely regardless
  of timing, and typically fall under standard warranty
- Report the moment you notice something — the gap between noticing and reporting is what
  actually costs you

## What changes once you've left the delivery center

At handover, an issue is unambiguous: it's there, on a car Tesla hasn't handed off yet, in
front of a delivery advisor who can note it on the spot. A week later, the same scratch raises
a question neither of you can definitively answer — was it there at delivery, or did it happen
in your driveway? That ambiguity, not a hard deadline, is the real cost of reporting late.

This matters more for cosmetic issues than functional ones. A scratch or a panel gap is a
one-time, static thing — its history matters. A warning light or a malfunctioning system is an
ongoing, present-tense thing — it's judged on whether it's happening *now*, and standard
warranty coverage generally applies regardless of exactly when you first noticed it.

## Is there an actual deadline?

Tesla has, at various points, offered a specific window after delivery to report cosmetic
issues without the same burden of proof — but the length and the specifics have changed over
time and vary by market. **Don't plan around a number you read somewhere** (including a
specific figure in a forum post); confirm the current policy for your region directly, either
in your delivery paperwork or by asking Tesla service. What's consistent everywhere: sooner is
always better than later, and same-day is always better than sooner.

## What to do right now, if you just noticed something

1. **Photograph it immediately** — the date on the photo file is itself part of your record.
2. **Write down what you remember about delivery day** — did you look at that spot? Would you
   have plausibly missed it? Being honest with yourself here helps you describe it accurately
   later.
3. **Report it the same day**, through the Tesla app, website, or by contacting a service
   center — don't wait to see if it's "worth mentioning." The gap between noticing and
   reporting is the thing that actually erodes your position, far more than the gap between
   delivery and noticing.
4. **Describe it factually**, not as an accusation — "I noticed X on this panel, here's a
   photo, can this be addressed" gets a more useful response than a complaint about the
   delivery process.

## How to talk about it with Tesla

Lead with the observation, not a theory about when it happened. "This is what I'm seeing" is a
much easier conversation than "you missed this at delivery" — the second framing invites a
debate you can't actually win with certainty, while the first simply asks for a fix. If it's a
functional or safety issue, say so plainly and ask for it to be prioritized; those get treated
differently from a cosmetic note regardless of timing.

## The lesson for next time (yours or someone else's)

If there's a next Tesla in your future — or if you're reading this before a delivery that
hasn't happened yet — the fix for this whole situation is upstream of it: a documented,
photographed pass through the car *at* delivery removes the ambiguity entirely. That's not
always possible in the rush of handover, which is exactly why a structured, guided checklist
like TSL Check exists — so the inspection happens methodically in the time you have, instead
of relying on catching everything by eye in a few rushed minutes.
