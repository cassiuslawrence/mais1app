Ask in any Tesla owners' group whether something is "normal" and you'll get twenty confident,
contradictory answers. That's not because nobody knows — it's because the honest answer is
usually "it depends," and nuance doesn't travel well in a comment thread. This guide is a
calibration pass: the things new-Tesla owners most often ask about, and where the real line
between *normal* and *worth flagging* actually sits.

None of this is about lowering your standards. It's about spending your attention on the
things that matter — asymmetry, persistence, and anything safety-related — instead of on
variation that exists in every factory-built car on the planet.

**TL;DR — the pattern that separates normal from not:**

- **Normal:** small and *even* imperfections, present on both sides equally
- **Worth flagging:** asymmetry — one side different from the other
- **Normal:** a quirk that clears on its own or after a software update
- **Worth flagging:** anything that persists or gets worse
- **Always worth flagging:** anything related to brakes, steering, restraints, or fire risk —
  when in doubt, don't wait

## Panel gaps

**Small and even is normal. Wide on one side, tight on the other, is not.** Every
factory-built car — Tesla, German luxury brands, everyone — has some panel gap variation; it's
a function of tolerances stacking up across a body made of dozens of parts. What's genuinely
worth a second look is *asymmetry*: a door gap noticeably wider at the top than the bottom, or
a hood/frunk gap clearly wider on one side than the other. That pattern points to something
actually misaligned, not just normal variation.

## Paint texture and finish

**Some orange peel is normal; heavy texture and drips are not.** "Orange peel" — a light,
dimpled texture in the clear coat — exists to some degree on most factory-painted cars. It's
the nature of spraying a liquid finish at production speed. What's worth documenting: texture
you can see clearly from more than a meter away, frozen drips along the lower edge of a bumper
or door, or a panel whose color or metallic flake doesn't quite match its neighbor.

## Road noise and ride firmness

**A firm ride and some road noise are part of the car's design, not a defect.** The Model Y and
Model 3 are tuned firmer than a typical gas sedan, and Performance trims with sportier tires
are noisier still — that's the car being what it's supposed to be. What *is* worth checking:
a new whistling noise at highway speed that wasn't there before, which usually traces back to a
door or hatch seal that's folded, pinched, or not compressing evenly.

## Rattles and interior noises

**Occasional light rattles on rough pavement happen in many new cars.** Trim panels settle in
during the first weeks of driving, and a light click here and there over a pothole isn't
unusual. A rattle that's loud, constant regardless of road surface, or traceable to the exact
same spot every time is a different story — that's more often a clip or fastener that isn't
fully seated, and it's a quick fix if you can point to where it's coming from.

## Software quirks and screen behavior

**A quirk that clears after a reboot or an update is normal; one that persists is not.**
Software-defined cars occasionally have small hiccups — a slow-to-load app, a setting that
resets, a message that appears once and never again. If dismissing it or restarting the car
makes it go away and it doesn't come back, that's the software equivalent of a factory
variation. If the same alert or behavior keeps recurring after updates, it's worth reporting
rather than living with.

## Charging and range display quirks

**Range estimates fluctuating with temperature and driving style is normal.** Cold weather,
highway speed, and aggressive driving all reduce real-world range versus the display — that's
physics, not a fault. What's not normal: the car failing to charge at all at a working
station, or a persistent charging-related warning that doesn't clear.

## The pattern that matters, restated

If you take one thing from this guide, make it this: **look for asymmetry and persistence, not
imperfection.** A car with zero variation anywhere doesn't exist. A car where one side is
visibly different from the other, or where something doesn't go away on its own, is worth
documenting and asking about — calmly, with photos, not as an accusation.

## And the exception to all of it: safety

Calibration goes out the window for anything touching brakes, steering, seatbelts, airbags, or
any smell or sign of overheating/burning. Those aren't "wait and see" items regardless of how
minor they seem — get them looked at.

## Turning "is this normal?" into a documented answer

The uncertainty itself is often the most stressful part — not knowing whether something is
worth mentioning leaves people either over-reporting everything or staying quiet about real
issues. Photographing what you see, at the moment you see it, turns a vague worry into
something concrete you can compare, show a delivery advisor, or simply file away as
confirmed-normal. That's the whole idea behind TSL Check's reference photos: not to diagnose
for you, but to give you something real to compare against instead of guessing from a forum
thread.
