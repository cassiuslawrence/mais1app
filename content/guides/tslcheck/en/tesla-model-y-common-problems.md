The Model Y is the best-selling car in the world, which means two things: Tesla builds a lot
of them, and the ones with problems are very visible online. Most Model Ys are fine. But a
few issues come up often enough — at delivery on a new car, and on the used market — that
it's worth knowing exactly what to look for before money changes hands.

This guide covers the problems Model Y buyers most commonly report, based on the inspection
checklist behind the TSL Check app. For each one: what it looks like, why it matters, and a
quick way to spot it. It also flags what changed with the **2025 "Juniper" refresh** and how
the **RWD, Long Range and Performance** variants differ. None of this is Tesla-bashing —
factory variation exists in every brand. It's about knowing what's normal, what isn't, and
catching the difference while it's still someone else's problem to fix.

**TL;DR — what Model Y buyers most often report:**

- Uneven panel gaps (doors, frunk, rear hatch)
- Condensation inside the tail lights
- Paint defects: thin edges, orange peel, runs, transport chips
- Wind noise from door and hatch seals
- Rear hatch alignment and water pooling
- Interior trim rattles, seat marks, screen dead pixels
- Juniper-specific: light bars, rear screen, early-build quirks
- Screen warnings that shouldn't be there at handover

## Uneven panel gaps

**The single most-reported Model Y issue, new or used.** Doors, frunk and the rear hatch
should sit with gaps that look consistent from side to side. A door gap noticeably wider at
the top than the bottom points to a door misaligned on its hinges; a frunk or hatch gap
clearly wider on one side than the other is a factory fit issue.

Why it matters: beyond looks, misaligned panels are the leading cause of wind noise at
highway speed, and they're the first thing a future buyer's eye lands on. Small, *even* gaps
are normal — asymmetry is the tell.

*Quick check: stand square to the front and compare the frunk gap left vs. right, then walk
each door comparing its gap top vs. bottom, and do the same at the rear hatch.*

## Condensation inside the tail lights

**A well-known Model Y complaint worth checking specifically.** A little temporary fogging
inside a light housing after a cold or wet night can be normal and clears on its own.
Persistent water droplets, or a visible pool in the bottom of the tail light, point to a
failed seal.

Why it matters: moisture sits next to electronics and reflectors, which over time means
corrosion and dimmer output — a warranty conversation that's far easier on day one than
months later.

*Quick check: look into each tail light (and the reversing lights) for droplets or a
waterline, especially first thing in the morning.*

## Paint: thin edges, orange peel and transport marks

**Some texture is normal — thin paint and drips are not.** "Orange peel" (a light dimpled
texture in the clear coat) exists to some degree on most factory-painted cars, Tesla
included. What's worth documenting: texture visible from more than a meter away, frozen
drips along the lower edges of bumpers and doors, chips from the protective transport film,
or a panel whose color or metallic flake doesn't match its neighbors (a possible repaint).

Thin paint on door edges and the underside of the hatch is a separately known Tesla issue —
exposed metal there can corrode over the years.

Why it matters: paint is a resale-value item, and edge corrosion is a slow problem that
starts the day you take delivery.

*Quick check: compare adjacent panels from about a meter away in daylight, then open each
door and the hatch and look along the edges.*

## Wind noise from seals

**A pinched or loose door seal is the classic source of Model Y wind noise.** The rubber
should run continuously around each opening and the hatch, and compress evenly when closed.
Sections that are folded, loose or torn will whistle at speed and can let water in during
heavy rain.

Why it matters: wind-noise complaints are among the most common Model Y service visits, and
the cause is often a seal that could have been spotted at handover.

*Quick check: run your eyes and fingers around the full length of each door seal and the
hatch seal.*

## Rear hatch alignment and water

**The tailgate is worth its own look.** Check that the hatch sits flush and its gaps are
even on both sides, that it closes without needing a slam, and — on a used car especially —
that there's no water pooling in the spare-wheel well or dampness in the boot liner, which
can point to a hatch seal or drainage issue.

Why it matters: a leak you find before buying is a negotiation; one you find after is a hunt
for the source and a wet boot.

*Quick check: open the hatch, lift the boot floor, and look and feel for water or damp in
the well.*

## Interior: trim, seats and screen

**A new car's interior should have no story to tell.** Look for trim panels that flex or
click under light finger pressure (not clipped in fully), marks on the seats, and — on the
center screen — dead pixels or discoloration, easiest to spot on a pure white or black
image. Rattles from the dash or doors on rough roads are the most-reported interior gripe.

Why it matters: interior defects are the ones you'll see and hear every single day, and
they're quick fixes at a delivery center but slow, appointment-based ones afterwards.

*Quick check: with the screen on a bright, uniform image, scan corner to corner; press
gently along trim edges; and note any rattle on your test drive.*

## What changed with the 2025 "Juniper" refresh

The refreshed Model Y ("Juniper") brought a full-width front light bar, a wraparound rear
light bar, a rear passenger touchscreen, acoustic glass, and revised suspension and sound
insulation. Two practical notes for buyers:

- **Early-build quirks.** As with any first-year revision, the earliest Junipers are the
  ones to inspect most carefully — software settling in, and the new rear screen and light
  bars are worth confirming all work as intended.
- **The light bars are polarizing, not a defect.** Plenty of owners love them; some don't.
  That's taste, not a fault — don't let a strong opinion online talk you out of confirming
  the car itself is sound.

*Quick check on a Juniper: cycle the front and rear light bars, test the rear screen's
climate and media controls, and confirm the software shows no pending updates or alerts.*

## RWD, Long Range and Performance: what differs

The variant changes what you're inspecting more than whether the car is sound:

- **Rear-Wheel Drive** — single motor, smaller battery, steel-sprung. Simplest to check;
  confirm the rated range matches what's advertised for the trim.
- **Long Range** — dual motor, the volume seller. Check that all-wheel drive engages and
  there are no drive-unit warnings.
- **Performance** — bigger brakes, stickier (and pricier) tires, lowered/firmer suspension,
  unique wheels. Inspect tire wear and brake condition closely; a Performance's consumables
  are a real invoice, and curb-rashed wheels are common.

A firmer ride and more road noise are characteristic of the Model Y (especially Performance)
— that's the car's design, not a defect.

## What's actually normal

Calibration, not paranoia. Generally considered normal on a Model Y: small and *even* panel
gaps, a light degree of orange peel, brief light condensation that clears, a firm ride, some
road and tire noise, and minor software quirks that clear after an update. The pattern that
matters is **asymmetry and persistence** — one side different from the other, or an issue
that doesn't go away.

## If you find something

On a **new** car, photograph anything you find before you sign, from an angle that shows both
the defect and its location, and report it to the delivery team while you're still there.
Tesla has historically allowed a short window to report cosmetic issues, but the specifics
have changed over time and vary by market — confirm the current policy at handover rather
than assuming one. On a **used** car, the same documented list is your negotiation: cosmetic
and wear items are numbers you can subtract; a leak, an undisclosed repaint, or paperwork
that doesn't line up are reasons to renegotiate or walk. Either way, the safest moment is
always the same: before you drive away.
