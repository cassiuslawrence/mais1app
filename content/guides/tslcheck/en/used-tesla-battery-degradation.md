If there's one number that decides whether a used Tesla is a good deal, it's not the odometer
— it's the battery. Every EV battery loses some capacity over time, that part isn't in
question. What buyers actually need to know is whether the loss on *this specific car* is
typical for its age and mileage, and whether the asking price already accounts for it. This
guide is about answering that with a number instead of a guess.

**TL;DR:**

- Some degradation is normal and expected — the question is whether *this car's* loss matches
  its age and mileage, not whether it exists at all
- Loss is typically faster in the first year or two, then flattens out
- Check it yourself: 100% charge, compare shown range to the original rated figure
- Never mix EPA and WLTP figures — they're different scales for the same car
- The battery is the single biggest value item on a used EV — verify it, don't trust the
  listing

## Why the battery matters more than anything else on the car

On a gas car, a used-car inspection is mostly about the engine and transmission — expensive,
wear-prone parts that are hard to verify from a listing. On an EV, the battery plays that
role, but with a difference: **range loss is permanent.** It doesn't get better with an oil
change or a tune-up. Whatever the degradation is on the day you buy the car is roughly what
you're living with (and reselling with) for as long as you own it — which is exactly why it's
worth a real check rather than trusting a seller's description.

## What degradation actually looks like over time

EV batteries generally follow a predictable pattern: **a comparatively faster dip in the first
year or two of ownership, then a much flatter curve for the rest of the car's life.** A
one-year-old car showing a few percent of range loss isn't unusual — that's the early part of
the curve, not a red flag on its own. What you're evaluating isn't "is there degradation"
(there is, on every EV), but whether the specific number lines up with what's typical for the
car's age and mileage.

## How to actually check it

1. Set the battery/range display to show **distance**, not percentage.
2. Charge the car to **100%** (or use the last time it was charged that high, if you can't
   charge it yourself before buying).
3. Note the estimated range shown at full charge.
4. Compare that number to the car's **original rated range** for its specific trim and model
   year — this is published by Tesla and searchable for the exact configuration you're
   looking at.
5. The percentage difference between those two numbers is your degradation estimate.

## Don't mix EPA and WLTP

This is the single most common way people get their own degradation math wrong. **EPA**
(used in the US) and **WLTP** (used in the EU, UK, and several other markets) are different
test cycles that produce different range figures for the *same car* — WLTP numbers are
typically higher than EPA numbers for an identical configuration. If you compare a car's
current range against the wrong rating system's original figure, you'll calculate degradation
that isn't real. Always confirm which system applies in your market, and use that one
consistently.

## What counts as "too much"

There's no single official cutoff, and Tesla's battery warranty terms (which vary by model
and market, and are worth checking directly for the specific car) are the closest thing to an
official standard — but as a practical buyer, the real question isn't a percentage in
isolation, it's **whether the price reflects it.** A car with more degradation than typical
for its age isn't automatically a bad buy; it's a car that should cost less. The negotiation
lever is the gap between "typical for this age/mileage" and what you're actually measuring.

## What else affects range that isn't degradation

Before you attribute a lower-than-expected range reading to degradation, rule out the
non-battery explanations: cold weather significantly reduces real-world range temporarily
(not permanently), tire choice and pressure affect efficiency, and aggressive prior driving
history doesn't damage the battery but can make range *feel* worse in daily use. None of
these show up in the 100%-charge range check above, which is exactly why that specific method
is the one to trust over a vague "it doesn't seem to go as far as it should."

## Turning this into a negotiation, not a guess

A degradation estimate you calculated yourself, with photos of the charge screen showing the
range and percentage, is a concrete number you can bring into a price conversation — much
stronger than a feeling that "the range seems low." Pair it with everything else on a
used-Tesla inspection (tires, brakes, 12V battery age, paperwork) for the full picture, but
the battery number is usually the one that moves the price most.
